# Personal Finance Guide

General reference information on budgeting, saving, debt, and investing basics. This is
educational material, not personalized financial advice.

## Budgeting

### The 50/30/20 Rule

A commonly used starting framework splits after-tax income into **50% needs** (housing,
utilities, groceries, minimum debt payments), **30% wants** (dining out, entertainment,
subscriptions), and **20% savings and extra debt payoff**. It's a rough guideline, not a
strict formula — high cost-of-living areas often need more than 50% for needs.

### Tracking Spending

Reviewing spending **monthly** against a budget, rather than only at tax time or year-end,
makes it much easier to catch categories that are quietly growing before they become a
problem.

## Emergency Fund

### How Much

A common guideline is **3–6 months of essential expenses** kept in an easily accessible
account (not invested in the stock market), sized toward the larger end for
single-income households or less stable employment.

### Where to Keep It

High-yield savings accounts are commonly recommended over a standard checking account for
an emergency fund, since they earn meaningfully more interest while remaining fully
liquid — unlike a CD (certificate of deposit), which locks money up for a fixed term.

## Debt Payoff Strategies

### Avalanche Method

Pay minimums on all debts, then put extra money toward the debt with the **highest
interest rate** first. This minimizes total interest paid over time and is the
mathematically optimal approach for most people.

### Snowball Method

Pay minimums on all debts, then put extra money toward the **smallest balance** first,
regardless of interest rate. This trades some extra interest cost for faster
psychological wins, which research suggests helps some people stick with a payoff plan
longer.

### Credit Card Interest

Credit card APRs commonly range from **18–29%**, which is high enough that paying off
credit card balances in full each month is almost always a better use of money than most
investment returns.

## Investing Basics

### Index Funds

An index fund holds a broad basket of stocks (e.g., tracking the S&P 500) rather than
picking individual companies. Because they're passively managed, they typically carry
much lower fees — often **under 0.10% annually** — than actively managed funds.

### Compound Growth

Money invested earns returns, and those returns then earn their own returns over time.
Starting **even 10 years earlier** with smaller contributions often outperforms starting
later with larger ones, purely due to the extra time compounding.

### Retirement Accounts

Employer-sponsored retirement accounts often include **employer matching** — free money
up to a certain percentage of salary — which is commonly recommended to capture in full
before investing elsewhere, since it's an immediate, guaranteed return that's hard to
beat with any other investment.

## Credit Scores

### What Affects It

The largest factors are typically **payment history** (paying on time) and **credit
utilization** (the percentage of available credit currently used) — utilization under
**30%**, and ideally under 10%, is commonly recommended for a healthy score.

### Common Score Ranges

Scoring models vary, but a widely used range (FICO) treats **670–739** as "good,"
**740–799** as "very good," and **800+** as "exceptional." Scores below **580** are
commonly classified as "poor" and can significantly affect loan approval and interest
rates offered.
